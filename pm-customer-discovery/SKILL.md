# Customer Discovery

Help PMs learn what customers actually need rather than collect confirmation for what they already plan to build. Every artifact this skill produces is designed around one principle (The Mom Test): **ask about the customer's life and past behavior, never about your idea or their future intentions.**

## Before starting

1. **Riskiest assumption** — what belief, if wrong, kills the initiative? Discovery should target that, not generally "learn about users". If the user can't name it, help them: usually it's "this problem is painful enough that people will change behavior/pay to fix it."
2. **Stage** — exploring a problem space (generative) vs. validating a specific problem/solution (evaluative)? Different instruments.
3. **Who and how many** — target segment, access to participants, timeline.

## Interview guide design

Structure every guide as a funnel:

1. **Context** (5 min) — role, workflow, where the problem space fits in their week
2. **Past-behavior stories** (20 min, the core) — "Tell me about the last time you [did the job]. Walk me through it." Then dig: What was hardest? What did you do about it? What did that cost you?
3. **Current solutions** (10 min) — what they use today, what they've tried and abandoned, what they cobbled together themselves (homegrown spreadsheets are the strongest demand signal that exists)
4. **Prioritization** (5 min) — "Of everything we discussed, what would you fix first? Why that one?"

Rules baked into every guide:
- Every question asks about a specific past instance, not generalities ("last time" not "usually")
- No question contains the user's solution idea — if validating a solution, that goes in a separate concept-test section at the END, after problem questions are done and clearly marked so the interviewer knows the signal quality drops there
- Include planned follow-up probes ("what did that cost you?", "who else was involved?", "what happened next?") — interviewers freeze without them
- Include a "listen for" sidebar: workarounds, budget already spent on the problem, emotional language, frequency words

Deliver guides with: a screener (3–5 questions to qualify participants), the guide itself, a note-capture template aligned to the guide sections, and a per-interview debrief form (top 3 surprises, evidence for/against the riskiest assumption, quotes worth keeping).

## JTBD analysis

When the user wants jobs-to-be-done framing, structure jobs as:

> When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome].

Map for each job: functional, emotional, and social dimensions; the full job map (define → locate → prepare → execute → monitor → resolve); where current solutions underserve; and the hiring/firing criteria (what would make someone fire their current solution — switching forces: push of the problem, pull of the new, anxiety of change, habit of the present).

## Survey design

Surveys measure frequency of what interviews discovered — never run a survey before interviews unless forced. When designing one: every question maps to a decision it informs; behavioral questions before attitudinal; no leading constructions; max 10 questions (state expected completion rate honestly); include one open-ended question max. Flag sampling bias in whatever distribution channel the user proposes.

## Personas

Build personas only from actual research data the user provides — refuse to invent demographic fiction. A useful persona is organized around: the job they're hiring for, current behavior, pain severity, buying power, and a "we win them when / we lose them when" line. If the user has no research data yet, offer **proto-personas** explicitly labeled as assumptions to be tested, with a test plan attached.

## Discovery sprint plan

For "we need to validate X in N weeks": produce a week-by-week plan with riskiest-assumption statement, method per assumption (interview/survey/concept test/fake door/data analysis), recruitment plan with screener, sample sizes with rationale (typically 5–8 interviews per segment per round — patterns stabilize, certainty doesn't), and pre-committed decision criteria: "we proceed if we hear [signal] from [threshold]; we stop if [signal]." Decision criteria written BEFORE data collection — that's what separates discovery from rationalization.

## Quality bar

- Zero questions that pitch the idea or ask "would you use/pay for…" (hypothetical-demand questions are banned; willingness-to-pay is inferred from current spend and workaround cost)
- Every instrument traces to the riskiest assumption
- Decision criteria pre-committed before fieldwork
- Sample guidance includes who NOT to talk to (friends, existing happy customers when testing new segments)
